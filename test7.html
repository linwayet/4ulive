七 閱讀測驗（題 65–68）Heart Disease
閱讀文章
While often associated with older generations, cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a global health concern that begins with lifestyle choices made as early as adolescence. To understand heart disease, one must first understand the heart's primary function: acting as a high-pressure pump that circulates oxygen-rich blood through a network of arteries.
The most common form of heart disease is Coronary Artery Disease (CAD). This condition is primarily driven by a process called atherosclerosis. Over time, the inner walls of the arteries can become damaged due to high blood pressure, smoking, or high cholesterol. When this damage occurs, the body attempts to repair it, leading to the accumulation of "plaque"—a sticky mixture of fat, cholesterol, and calcium.
As plaque builds up, the arteries narrow and harden, a process known as stenosis. This restricts the flow of blood to the heart muscle. If a piece of plaque ruptures, it can trigger a blood clot that completely blocks the artery. Without oxygen, the heart muscle begins to die, resulting in a myocardial infarction, or a heart attack.
Scientific research distinguishes between risk factors that we can change and those we cannot. Non-modifiable factors are inherent traits such as genetics, advancing age, and biological sex. In contrast, modifiable factors represent lifestyle choices within a person's control, including dietary habits, physical activity levels, and tobacco use.
In Taiwan, health experts have observed that the shift toward "Westernized" diets—characterized by high intake of processed sugars and saturated fats—is a major contributor to rising obesity and hypertension rates among the youth. Fortunately, the progression of atherosclerosis can often be slowed or prevented by adopting a fiber-rich diet, engaging in regular aerobic exercise, and avoiding nicotine, which is known to constrict blood vessels and accelerate arterial damage.

八 混合題（題 69–73）Oral Health
閱讀文章
For decades, many people viewed oral health as a separate entity from general medicine. However, modern science has increasingly shown that the mouth is the primary gateway to the rest of the body, and its condition often serves as a window into an individual's overall health. From cardiovascular issues to systemic inflammation, the biological links between our gums and our organs are far more profound than previously understood.
In a healthy mouth, thousands of bacteria coexist in a delicate balance. However, when oral hygiene is neglected, these bacteria form a sticky, complex structure known as biofilm (plaque). If not removed through regular brushing and flossing, this biofilm triggers an immune response. This leads to gingivitis—the first stage of gum disease—characterized by redness, swelling, and bleeding.
The real danger arises when gingivitis progresses into periodontitis. In this stage, the infection breaks down the bone and connective tissue that hold teeth in place. More alarmingly, the chronic inflammation in the gums allows oral bacteria to enter the bloodstream. Once circulating, these pathogens can contribute to the formation of arterial plaques, significantly increasing the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke.
The relationship between oral health and diabetes is particularly noteworthy as a "two-way street." People with diabetes are more susceptible to severe gum disease because they are generally more prone to infections. Conversely, severe gum disease can make it more difficult for patients to control their blood sugar levels. Inflammation in the mouth seems to impair the body's ability to utilize insulin, creating a dangerous cycle that affects the patient's entire metabolic system.
Another overlooked aspect of oral health is the critical role of saliva. Saliva is not just water; it is a complex fluid containing enzymes, proteins, and minerals like calcium and phosphate. It acts as a primary defense mechanism by neutralizing acids produced by bacteria and physically washing away food particles. Conditions that cause "dry mouth" (xerostomia), often a side effect of certain medications, can lead to a rapid increase in tooth decay because this natural protective shield is missing.
Ultimately, the myth that "it's just a cavity" or "bleeding gums are normal" is dangerous. Maintaining oral hygiene is not merely about a bright smile; it is a fundamental pillar of preventative medicine. By understanding that the mouth is an integral part of the body's ecosystem, we can make more informed choices that protect our long-term vitality.
